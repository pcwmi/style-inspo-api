# RESEARCH: Buying-Intent Queries — Volume, Intent & Conversion Potential

**Date**: 2026-05-20
**Status**: Research / strategy input
**Question**: When people ask "should I buy this?", how big is that moment, how
strong is the intent, and can it (a) drive affiliate revenue and (b) be a growth
channel for Style Inspo?

---

## TL;DR — Bottom Line

The hypothesis has **three separate claims**. The research grades them differently:

| Claim | Verdict | Why |
|-------|---------|-----|
| 1. "Should I buy this?" is a real, large, high-stakes moment | ✅ **Strongly validated** | 85% of apparel buyers research online first; clothing is the #1 returned category (up to 40%); a competitor's "Should I Buy This?" button hit 500K downloads in <6 months |
| 2. That moment can convert to affiliate revenue | ✅ **Validated, mechanically** | Buying-intent ("review", "worth it", "vs", "should I buy") content converts **2–5× higher** than informational content. But there's a structural trust conflict — see §6 |
| 3. Buying-intent queries are a **growth/acquisition channel** | ⚠️ **Weakest link** | The query itself is high-intent but **low-volume per query** and **fragmented long-tail**. It's a great *retention + revenue* surface, a poor *standalone acquisition* surface. Growth has to come from the social/shareable layer, not from people Googling "should I buy X" and finding you |

**The reframe**: "Should I buy this?" is best understood as a **bottom-of-funnel
revenue + retention moment**, not a top-of-funnel acquisition channel. Treat it
as the place you *monetize and build trust with users you already have* — and
get acquisition from the shareable social artifact it produces.

---

## 1. The Hypothesis, Unpacked

The original hypothesis bundles three things that should be evaluated separately:

1. **The moment exists** — people genuinely pause and ask "should I buy this?"
2. **Monetization** — that moment is where affiliate revenue can be earned
3. **Growth** — people are "reaching for content like this", so it pulls in new users

Claims 1 and 2 are about **revenue and product value**. Claim 3 is about
**distribution**. They have very different answers, so they're treated separately
below.

---

## 2. Is the "Should I Buy This?" Moment Real & Large? — YES

### Pre-purchase research is now near-universal

- **81%** of all consumers research online before buying; **85%** specifically
  before an *apparel* purchase.
- **54%** rank search engines as their #1 pre-purchase source; Amazon 35%,
  individual retailer sites 33%.
- **73%** use multiple channels when researching a purchase.
- **76%** say social content influenced a purchase in the last 6 months.
- **88%** "webroom" — compare online before committing.

The deliberation step is no longer a niche behavior; it is the default. Almost
every apparel purchase now has a research phase, and that phase is where Style
Inspo's "Buy Smarter" feature lives.

### The cost of *bad* buying decisions is huge — and visible

This is the strongest argument that the moment *matters*, not just that it
exists:

- Clothing is the **#1 most-returned online category** — **26%** of US consumers
  returned online clothing in 2025; online apparel return rates run **up to 40%**.
- **Impulse buying + buyer's remorse** drives ~**11%** of returns outright.
- **57%** of women's online impulse buys are clothing.
- "Bracketing" (buy multiple, return most): **62%** of shoppers; **51%** of Gen Z.

Every one of those returns is a "should I buy this?" question that was answered
badly. The pain is real, expensive, and felt by the user personally (closet
clutter, wasted money, guilt). That is fertile ground for a tool that improves
the decision.

### Direct market validation: a competitor literally shipped this button

**Phia** (founded by Phoebe Gates & Sophia Kianni) ships a feature called
**"Should I Buy This?"** — it compares the item you're viewing against real-time
prices across 40,000+ retail and resale sites. **It hit 500K+ downloads in under
6 months.** That is the cleanest possible proof that the moment is real and that
users will install a dedicated tool for it.

**Conclusion on Claim 1:** The moment is real, universal, expensive when done
badly, and already market-validated. Not in doubt.

## 3. Query Volume & Intent — How Big, How Specific?

This is the heart of the user's question. The key finding is a **classic
volume-vs-intent tradeoff**.

### The shape of the demand (head vs long tail)

There is no single high-volume "should I buy" keyword. The demand is shaped like
a long tail:

| Query type | Example | Relative volume | Intent strength | Commercial value |
|------------|---------|-----------------|-----------------|------------------|
| Generic head term | "should I buy this" | Low (as a literal phrase) | Vague | Low |
| Informational | "how to style a blazer", "outfit ideas" | **Very high** | Weak | Low (0.3–1.2% conv.) |
| Commercial investigation | "is the [brand] [coat] worth it", "[item] review", "[brand] vs [brand]" | **Moderate per query, enormous in aggregate** | **Strong** | **High (2.5–6% conv.)** |
| Transactional | "buy [item]", "[item] discount code" | Moderate | **Strongest** | Highest (3–9% conv.) |

The volume Style Inspo cares about ("should I buy *this specific item*") lives in
the **commercial-investigation long tail**: thousands of micro-queries —
`is the Sézane Gaspard worth it`, `Quince cashmere review`, `Reformation vs
Sézane` — each individually small, collectively massive.

### The core principle: specificity *is* the value

Industry consensus from the affiliate/SEO data is blunt:

> "Volume matters less than targeting the right intent. Ten review posts
> targeting buyer-intent keywords will out-earn 50 general informational posts."

> "A post ranking for 'is X worth it' will almost always out-earn a post ranking
> for 'what is X'."

So the answer to *"how specific does it need to be to convert?"* is: **the more
specific, the better.** A vague "should I buy something" has no commercial value.
"Should I buy *this $128 Sézane wool coat*" is a near-converted sale. The
specificity isn't a problem to be solved — it's the entire reason the moment
converts.

### Honest caveat on hard numbers

Exact monthly search volumes for buying-intent fashion queries require a paid
keyword tool (Ahrefs, Semrush, Google Keyword Planner) and weren't obtainable in
this research pass. What *is* well-established:

- "Should I buy" / "is it worth it" are **commercial-investigation /
  bottom-of-funnel (BOFU)** queries — known to be lower-volume, higher-intent.
- "Outfit ideas" / "how to style" are **much higher volume but informational** —
  weak commercial intent.
- Adjacent terms with real volume: `[item] review`, `[brand] honest review`,
  `best [category]` — these are large and convertible.

**Recommended next step to get real numbers** — see §7.

## 4. Can It Convert? — YES (the mechanics are excellent)

### Conversion rates by intent (the decisive data)

| Content / query intent | Conversion rate |
|------------------------|-----------------|
| Informational ("what is X", "how does X work") | 0.3–1.2% |
| **Comparison ("X vs Y", "best X for…")** | **2.5–6%** |
| **Review / "is it worth it"** | **3–8%** |
| Transactional ("buy X", "X review", "X discount") | 3–9% |
| Baseline fashion e-commerce | 2.9–3.3% (fast fashion 4–5%, luxury 1.5–2%) |
| AI-search-referred traffic | **14.2%** (vs Google organic 2.8%) |

Buying-intent content converts **2–5× better** than informational content. The
"should I buy this?" user is not cold traffic — they're standing on a product
page with a pre-formed intent to buy. Style Inspo isn't *creating* demand at that
moment; it's *arbitrating* an already-hot decision. That's the easiest kind of
conversion there is.

### Affiliate economics (fashion)

- **Commission rates**: 8–15% typical for apparel; creator platforms pay more —
  LTK averages 16%, ShopMy ranges 10–30%.
- **Market**: affiliate marketing >$37B in 2025, growing ~10%/yr; the creator
  affiliate economy alone is ~$17B.
- **ROI benchmark**: brands see ~$15 revenue per $1 spent on affiliates.

### Worked example — revenue per "Buy Smarter" session

Assume:
- Avg considered item: **$100** (the Buy Smarter spec itself uses an $89 example)
- Commission: **10%** → **$10** per completed purchase
- Because intent is pre-formed, the "conversion" here behaves more like
  cart-completion than cold acquisition — call it **25–40%** of sessions where
  the user does buy *something* (the original item or a recommended alternative)

→ **Effective EPC ≈ $2.50–$4.00 per Buy Smarter session.**

For context, typical display/affiliate EPCs are $0.05–$0.50. This moment is
**5–50× richer per interaction** than ordinary affiliate traffic — *if* the
click-out is captured (see §6 implementation note).

**Conclusion on Claim 2:** Mechanically, this is one of the highest-value
moments in all of affiliate marketing. The economics are not the risk. The
*trust conflict* is — see §6.

## 5. The Growth-Channel Question — The Weakest Part of the Hypothesis

The user explicitly flagged uncertainty here: *"one step of the hypothesis is
that people are reaching for content like this as a way for us to grow."* That
instinct to doubt it is correct. Three reasons "should I buy" is a **poor
standalone acquisition channel**:

1. **It's fragmented long-tail.** There's no single keyword to rank for or
   campaign to buy. Acquisition channels need concentration; this demand is
   spread across thousands of micro-queries.

2. **The intermediary layer is being eaten by AI.** ChatGPT Shopping, Perplexity,
   Google AI Overviews, and **Phia/Daydream** are positioning themselves as *the*
   place you ask "should I buy this?". A user Googling a buying-intent query in
   2026 increasingly gets an AI answer, not a list of tools to install. Note:
   AI-search traffic converts at 14.2% — the intent is fantastic, but the
   *gateway* is consolidating into a few apps.

3. **The user needs your tool already open.** "Should I buy this?" happens on a
   retailer's product page. For Style Inspo to be in that moment, the user must
   already have it installed / bookmarked / shared-to. That's a *retention*
   precondition, not an *acquisition* mechanism.

### Where growth actually comes from

The "people reach for this content" instinct *is* real — but the reaching
happens on **social**, not search:

- "TikTok made me buy it", "is it worth the hype", and **dupe** content are among
  the largest content genres on the platform — buying-intent content is
  *inherently shareable*.
- 76% of consumers were influenced by social content in the last 6 months.

So the growth loop is: **a user runs "should I buy this?" → gets a crisp,
screenshot-worthy verdict ("You own 2 of these — skip it" / "This fills a real
gap — here's 3 outfits") → shares it.** The *output artifact* is the growth
engine, not the query. This means the feature should be designed so its result
is **shareable by default** (clean verdict card, before/after, savings counter).

**Conclusion on Claim 3:** "Should I buy this?" is a **revenue + retention**
surface. Acquisition comes from making its *output* shareable. Don't budget for
it as an SEO/search acquisition play.

## 6. The Core Tension: Affiliate Incentive vs. Trust

This is the most important strategic risk and it is *not* in the original
hypothesis.

**Style Inspo's value proposition is honesty:** "You already own 2 of these —
don't buy it." (See `SPEC-buy-smarter.md` — the feature literally tracks
`total_saved` from purchases the user *didn't* make.) User research already shows
this is the real job: Dimple uses the product for **purchase validation**, and
the savings tracker is a core trust signal.

**Affiliate revenue pays you only when the user buys.** These incentives are in
direct opposition. If users sense the "should I buy?" answer is biased toward
"yes" because you earn on "yes", the trust — and the entire differentiator —
collapses.

### How to hold both (the Wirecutter model)

Wirecutter earns ~$20M+/yr in affiliate revenue *while being trusted* by keeping
**editorial and revenue strictly separated** — recommendations are made
regardless of whether an affiliate relationship exists. Style Inspo's equivalent:

- The **verdict** (buy / skip / you-already-own-this) must be computed with **zero
  affiliate input**. Never let commission rates touch the recommendation.
- Monetize the **"yes" path honestly**: when the genuine answer is "this fills a
  real gap", *that's* where the affiliate "Buy it here" link belongs.
- Monetize the **"no" path too**: "Skip this — but here's the actual gap in your
  closet" → affiliate link on the *better* alternative. The user is grateful AND
  you earn. This turns honesty into a revenue path instead of a revenue leak.
- Keep the **savings counter prominent** — it's the proof you're not just
  upselling.

### Implementation note: you must own the click-out

Affiliate attribution requires the purchase click to pass through *your*
affiliate link within the cookie window. If a user pastes a product URL into
Style Inspo, gets advice, then switches back to their own browser tab to buy —
**you earn nothing.** The feature must end with a tracked **"Buy it here →"**
button (via LTK/ShopMy/Skimlinks/CJ or direct retailer programs). Without that,
Claim 2 is true in theory and $0 in practice.

## 7. How to Get Real Query-Volume Numbers (Recommended Next Steps)

This research establishes the *structure* of the demand. To size it precisely:

1. **Pull keyword data** — Google Keyword Planner (free), or Ahrefs/Semrush
   (trial). Check volumes for:
   - `[brand] review`, `is [brand] worth it`, `[brand] vs [brand]` for ~10 brands
     in your users' consideration set (Sézane, Quince, Reformation, Everlane,
     Zara, etc.)
   - `worth the hype`, `[item] honest review`
   - Compare against `outfit ideas` / `how to style` to see the volume gap.
2. **Mine your own first-party data** — you already have signal:
   - PostHog: how many sessions hit the consider-buying flow? (events around the
     7 consider-buy primitives)
   - `buying_decisions.json` stats: `total_considered`, `total_bought`,
     `total_passed`, `total_saved` — this is your real-world conversion + intent
     data, uncorrupted by keyword-tool estimates.
3. **Instrument the decision split** — track buy / skip / later rates. If "skip"
   is high, the honesty value is proven but affiliate yield is lower → lean on
   the "here's the better alternative" monetization path.
4. **Test shareability** — add a share action to the verdict and measure whether
   shared verdicts drive installs. That directly tests Claim 3.

---

## 8. Summary & Recommendation

- **The moment is real and valuable** — don't re-litigate Claim 1. Apparel
  buying is high-research, high-regret, and a competitor proved demand (Phia,
  500K downloads).
- **Query volume is long-tail, not head-term.** The volume per query is modest;
  the *specificity is the feature, not a bug* — specific buying-intent queries
  convert 2–5× better than anything else.
- **Conversion economics are excellent** — effective EPC of ~$2.50–$4 per
  session vs $0.05–$0.50 for ordinary affiliate traffic — **but only if you own
  the click-out** and only if you protect trust.
- **Reframe the growth claim**: "should I buy this?" is a **bottom-funnel
  revenue + retention** surface, not a search-acquisition channel. Growth comes
  from making the *verdict shareable*.
- **The real risk is the trust/affiliate conflict.** Adopt the Wirecutter split:
  verdict computed with zero affiliate influence; monetize *both* the honest
  "yes" and the honest "no" (alternative-item link); keep the savings counter
  loud.

**One-line strategic takeaway:** Buying-intent is the right place to *earn* and
to *retain* — build the affiliate click-out and the shareable verdict card — but
do not count on the query itself to *acquire*. Acquisition is a social/shareable
problem, not a search-volume one.
